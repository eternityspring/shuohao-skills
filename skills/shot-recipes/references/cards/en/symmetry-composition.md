## What it is

The frame mirrors itself across a central axis: a corridor, a bridge, a boat cabin, double doors, two rows of seats. **The difference from centred framing is jurisdiction**: centred only asks that the subject sit in the middle and lets the background do whatever it likes; symmetry asks that the two halves match, which makes the background half the work.

The one-line test: **pin only the subject and it is centred framing; pin the background with it and it is symmetry.**

Symmetry is the cheapest way to say "this frame was designed". Nobody needs to know composition to feel the deliberateness — and the deliberateness is itself the meaning: order, ritual, pressure, unreality. Translated into words a model obeys: `symmetrical composition` only registers as "tidier". Write what sits on each side, **how many of them**, and that the camera looks straight down the axis. **Counts have to be pinned — matching shapes with mismatched counts leave the audience certain something is wrong and unable to say what.**

## When to use it

- **Ritual and authority.** An interrogation table, a funeral hall, a long boardroom table, an awards dais. Put the camera on the axis and the order grows straight out of the composition.
- **Pressure and entrapment.** The end of a corridor, the aisle between two rows of shelving, the gangway between two benches in a cabin. The subject is pinched between the halves and the frame has already said "there is nowhere to go".
- **The establisher that opens a scene.** One strictly symmetrical frame, then cut to the asymmetrical coverage. The contrast is at its strongest and you save a beat of exposition.
- **The cut where order breaks.** Frame one strictly symmetrical, frame two lets the subject walk off the axis. Those two cuts together are clearer than any line of dialogue.
- **Head-on symmetrical products.** Bottles, jars, earbud cases — things that are already mirror-symmetrical want this as their hero framing.

**When not to use it**:

- **Not in dialogue.** Arrange two people symmetrically and nobody can read who has the upper hand; the reverses that follow are wasted.
- **Not in scenes with direction.** Chases, running, travelling — symmetry is a static grammar and it fights motion on principle.
- **Not when the location is not symmetrical.** Piled clutter, open country. Force it and the model invents a mirrored side that does not exist.
- **Not twice in a row.** The whole effect lives in rarity; the second use leaves nothing behind but a corporate-video sheen.

## How to prompt it

One cut is the norm. Pin the counts on both sides; never just write "symmetrical".

```
wide shot, mirrored left and right halves（what sits on each side, named item by
item, with matching counts stated）, centre axis runs straight up the frame（what
is on the axis: the end of the corridor / the subject / a lamp）, what the subject
does on the axis, camera square to the axis and not angled, even light both sides
```

- **Matching counts beat matching shapes.** Three benches on the left means three on the right. Without numbers the model paints three and four, and the audience feels the wrongness immediately.
- **The camera must be square to the axis.** Add `the camera looks straight down the axis, not angled`, or the model reaches for its habitual slightly-oblique setup and the symmetry collapses on contact.
- **Light symmetrically too.** Side light breaks the mirror. Write `even light on both sides` and put the key on the axis — the window at the end of the corridor, the lamp directly overhead.
- **In 9:16 symmetry is easier to achieve and easier to fake.** A narrow frame holds less on each side, so the model's error rate drops by itself; the cost is that vertical symmetry drifts almost automatically into passport-photo stiffness. The fix is to push the axial subject down into the bottom third and give the top two thirds to the mirrored location — cabin roof, archway, tree canopy, roof beams. Never let the figure land exactly halfway up.
- **The `[Shot k]` passage carries the subject's action on the axis.** `Static Shot` is safest; if it must move, `Push In` or `Pull Out` along the axis. Any lateral move and the symmetry is gone on the first frame.
- **Leave one or two small differences.** Perfect symmetry reads as poster art; an extra lamp on one side, a coat hanging on the other, and the frame becomes believable again.

## Parameters

| Knob | Default | Range | How it feels when you tune it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strictness | roughly symmetrical | strict – rough | Strict looks the most designed and the most artificial; one or two deliberate differences read as photographed, at some cost to the ritual |
| Axial subject's share of height | one half | one third to three quarters | Smaller makes the flanking location more oppressive; at three quarters the symmetry is no longer visible |
| Elements per side | 2–3 | 1–4 | One per side is too thin to read as mirrored; past four the model loses count between the sides |
| Camera offset from the axis | 0 | 0 – 5% of width | A touch off feels more natural; past 5% it reads as misaligned, which looks far worse than committed asymmetry |
| Seconds per cut | 4s | 2–6s | Symmetrical frames hold well and can run to 6s — but use one or two per scene at most before it cloys |

## Known pitfalls

| Symptom | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Miscount | Three benches left, four right; it looks wrong and nobody can say why | Pin the count on each side and state that the counts match |
| Oblique camera | You asked for square-on and got a slight angle that flattens the whole effect | Add `the camera looks straight down the axis, not angled` |
| One bright side | Left lit, right in shadow, and the light breaks the mirror | State even illumination on both sides and put the key on the axis |
| Invented half | The location only ever had one side furnished and the model built a matching row on the other | Do not force symmetry onto asymmetrical locations; if you must, pin both sides completely |
| Passport photos | Several symmetrical cuts in a row and the scene reads like a promo, not drama | Use it as an accent, and break the axis in the very next cut |

## Examples

*The Letter Back* has no deliberately symmetrical frame in it. Across all thirty-six cuts the nearest thing is R26, where the wainscot line happens to cross at the same height either side of the flask — but that cut pins the subject in the middle and never asks the halves to mirror. It is centred framing, not this card.

**The cut with the strongest case for symmetry is R09**: the wide of the room, the son standing at the left end of the round table, the father seated at the right end, the letter, the flask and the mug between them. Move the camera off the side of the table and onto its axis, square to the blue glass window in the back wall, and the symmetry is already there — the window closes the axis, the round table opens into a true circle on it, the two men take half each, and the bare bulb hangs directly over the centre line. Write the light as even on both sides and "two men at either end of one letter" stops being a line of dialogue and becomes the composition.

The film does not shoot it that way, for two reasons. The sequence runs straight into the over-the-shoulder pair at R11 and R12, and symmetry levels the two men so nobody can tell who is waiting for whom to speak. And R09 already has one man standing and one man seated — an unequal posture that a mirror would flatten out. **Symmetry costs more than this scene can pay, which is this card's "not in a dialogue scene" rule showing up in the field.**
