## What it is

Head and shoulders fill the frame; the bottom edge lands somewhere between the collarbone and the chest. **The line against a medium is the hands**: hands still in frame is a medium, hands out of frame is a close. **The line against an extreme close is wholeness**: a close holds one complete face, an extreme close cannot even hold that.

The one-line test: **the only thing this cut asks the audience to read is the change on a face — that is a close.**

This card covers closes on people. Closes on objects are a different job, handled by `insert-beat` and `hands-tell`.

## When to use it

- **On the listener, right after a hard line.** The classic placement. The reaction outranks the line itself; what the audience came for is the half-second of freeze.
- **The moment of decision.** The eyes move before the body does, and a close is what catches the eyes moving first.
- **Lying and withholding.** A close shows the audience the thing the other character in the scene cannot see, so they know one fact more than the room does.
- **The emphasis line in talking-head.** Drop one close into a run of mediums and that sentence automatically weighs more.
- **The landing of a rising beat.** Medium, medium, close — the third cut lands on the face and the escalation finally pays off.

**When not to use it**:

- **Not to deliver information.** Who is where, how many people, what kind of room — a close answers none of it.
- **Never three or more closes in a row.** The audience loses the space and starts drifting out of the scene. Return to a medium every third or fourth cut to put people back in the room.
- **Not on someone walking.** Track a walker in close and the head wanders around the frame while the neck quietly stretches.
- **Not on a character with no design sheet.** A close is the exam for faces; with no reference the identity will change, and it changes visibly.

## How to prompt it

One cut is the norm, two for a reverse. Write the expression as one concrete change, never as an emotion adjective.

```
close-up, head and shoulders fill the frame, eyes on the upper third,
where the bottom edge cuts（collarbone / upper chest）, the one change happening on
the face（where the eyes land, the corner of the mouth, the breath）,
facing and eyeline（toward the person on frame left / out the window）,
lighting ratio and direction, a single soft background layer, cinematic film still
```

- **`eyes on the upper third` is doing composition work.** The model's reflex is to centre the face, and a centred face has no room to breathe — it looks like a filtered passport photo.
- **Write the expression as one change**: `the grin stiffening`, `the pupils tightening once`, not `looking shocked`. Adjectives get you a meme face.
- **Pin the eyeline.** At this size the environment is gone and the eyeline is the audience's only spatial cue — who they are looking at and from which side is what stitches this cut to the last one.
- **Keep the background to one layer.** Crowd a close's background and the model has to cram detail into a tiny area, which comes back as noise.
- Give `Static Shot`; to add pressure, a very slow `Push In` of no more than 10%.

## Parameters

| Knob | Default | Range | How it feels when you tune it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom cut line | collarbone | under the chin – chest | Higher (under the chin) is oppressive and approaches an extreme close; lower (at the chest) leaves some body in and eases the emotion one notch |
| Seconds per cut | 3s | 2–4s | A silent reaction is done in 2s; with dialogue, past 4s the features begin to drift |
| Eye height | upper third | upper quarter – centre | Higher tightens the headroom and raises the pressure; centred, and the frame instantly becomes a passport photo |
| Lighting ratio | 3:1 | 2:1 – 5:1 | Steeper reads as someone with something to hide, half the face sinking away; flat light turns a close into a plastic mask |
| Background blur | medium | light – strong | Some blur saves invention budget; blur it to nothing and the audience forgets where this cut is happening |

## Known pitfalls

| Symptom | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passport photo | Face dead centre, equal air on both sides, the frame goes inert | `eyes on the upper third`, then pin the facing and the eyeline |
| Identity swap | This cut's face is not the same person as the last cut's | Attach the character sheet, hold under 4s, and put the identifiers — hair, collar, mole, scar — into the prompt |
| Meme face | You asked for shock and got a gaping mouth and bulging eyes | Write one concrete change (the grin stiffens, the pupils tighten) instead of an emotion adjective |
| Floating lip sync | On a close with dialogue the mouth does not match the line | Prefer closes on the listener; give the speaker a medium, or move the line to voice-over |
| Edge crowding | The top of the head is sliced off, or the chin sits on the bottom edge | Write the headroom and the bottom cut line together in the same prompt |

## Examples

No cut in *The Letter Back* is filed under this card, but the reel uses the close seven times, and the clearest of them is R13: the father listening, 3s, locked off, mouth shut throughout, one movement in the entire cut — his eyes going down to the letter and coming back up.

Geometrically it sits exactly between the two boundaries. **The hands have dropped out of frame** — the hand lying flat on the table back in R11 is simply gone here — so it is a close and not a medium. **The face is still whole**, so it is not an extreme close like R21 either. Head and shoulders fill the frame, eyes on the upper third.

What is worth copying is the restraint: **no camera move at all.** The rig is locked for three seconds and the entire motion budget goes to one glance down and back. The reel's only close with a move is R14, the slow push on the son's face — and what that push is carrying is the decision itself. **Save close-plus-push for the cut where the emotion genuinely lifts; pin down all the others the way R13 does.**

One boundary while we are here: R19, R20, R22 and R27 are closes too, but their subject is the letter or the enamelware on the table, not a person. Closes on objects belong to cards like `insert-beat` and `hands-tell`, not to this one.
