## What it is

The source sits directly over the subject. Highlights land on the crown, the bridge of the nose, the cheekbones and the shoulder line, while the eye sockets, the area under the nose and the area under the chin all drop away together. **The thing it gets confused with is a high camera angle, and the two change completely different things**: a high angle moves the camera, so the audience looks down at the subject. Top light moves the light — the camera can sit at eye level all day, and only the shadowed parts of the face change.

The one-line test: **move where the audience is looking from and it is a high angle; move where the face goes dark and it is top light.**

Top light has an everyday counterpart everybody recognises: the noon sun. So the same lesson applies here as on `side-light` — **"high noon sun directly overhead" beats "top light" by a wide margin.** The first maps onto a mass of real photographs; the second is a term the model is only half sure about.

## When to use it

- **Exterior scenes that are actually set at midday.** A drying yard, a dock, a market street — at that hour top light is a physical fact, and leaving it out is what looks fake.
- **Interrogations.** A single bulb hanging over the table is the cheapest and most legible way to shoot the scene; the audience reads "somebody is being worked on" in one frame.
- **The moment someone is worn down.** Head lowered, sitting still, eye sockets dark and shoulder line bright: exhaustion and defeat arrive without any acting.
- **Overhead flat lays for product.** The default position for that composition, because it renders the object's outline and its contact shadow on the surface in the same pass.
- **Picking one person out of a crowd.** A dark room and a pool of light from above lifts your lead out of everyone else inside a single cut.

**When not to use it**:

- **Never on a talking head that needs to look good and trustworthy.** It blackens the eye sockets and deepens every nasolabial fold and under-eye bag. There is no less flattering position.
- **Not on two-handers that need intimacy.** Top light is inherently the light of being examined from above, and it fights closeness head on.
- **Not on vertical close-ups.** The eyes disappear into shadow on a phone, the audience loses the eyeline, and any reaction beat dies with it.
- **Not on a cut that already has a low camera angle.** Low angle plus top light gives a bright chin, black eyes and upturned nostrils, and the face falls apart.

## How to prompt it

One or two cuts. Top light is identified by where the highlights sit, not by the shadows, so name the bright patches on the crown and shoulders explicitly.

```
medium shot, light comes from directly above（what the overhead source is: high noon
sun directly overhead / a single bulb hanging from the cabin roof — name it）,
eye sockets stay readable（the whites and irises still show; not two black pits）,
where the highlights fall on crown, nose bridge, cheekbones and shoulder line,
which way the shadows under the nose and chin fall, camera at eye level,
background brightness and color temperature
```

- **Give a clock time or a physical fixture.** `high noon sun directly overhead` and `a single bare bulb hanging above the table` both work; `top light` mostly does not.
- **The eye sockets are the one place that must keep detail.** All the risk on this card concentrates there, which is why it gets its own required phrase.
- **Put the camera in its own clause.** Without `camera at eye level` the model lifts the camera overhead too — in its associations, top light and looking down are welded together.
- **Name each shadow.** In the sockets, under the nose, under the chin. Get those three disagreeing with each other and the face reads as fake.
- Use it on single figures. Top light on a group gives you a row of black-eyed faces nobody can read.
- Default `Static Shot`; `Push In` for an interrogation.

## Parameters

| Knob | Default | Range | How it feels when you tune it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ratio | 3 stops | 2–4 | At two the top-light flavour thins out into ordinary high key light; past four the sockets go solid black and all that is left of the face is a glowing scalp |
| Source offset | 15° forward of vertical | 0° overhead – 30° forward | Dead overhead always collapses the sockets; 15° forward is "top light you can still see eyes in"; by 30° you have a conventional high key and the tone is gone |
| Detail left in the sockets | whites and irises visible | irises visible – a single catchlight | Down is harsher and more oppressive, but past "a single catchlight" the audience loses the eyeline and reaction beats stop working |
| Color temperature | 5600K noon daylight | 3200K – 6500K | 2700–3200K for an interior bulb, 5600K for a midday exterior. Put the number in the prompt |
| Seconds per cut | 3s | 2–5s | Features are already carrying less information here; past 5s the shadow areas drift and the face slowly becomes somebody else |

## Known pitfalls

| Symptom | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Black pits | Two solid dark holes where the eyes are; no telling where they look | `eye sockets stay readable` must be there, and move the source 15° forward |
| Stage light | A round beam pressing straight down on the head, like a musical number | Cut `spotlight`, `beam`, `stage light`; describe the fixture instead (hanging bulb, skylight, noon sun) |
| Camera follows | You asked for top light and the model raised the camera overhead as well | Give the camera its own clause, `camera at eye level`, and keep light and camera in separate sentences |
| Plastic scalp | A dead white highlight on the crown and hair that looks like a moulded shell | Drop a stop and specify hair with visible strands |
| Ten years older | Folds, bags and chin shadows all show up and the character ages a decade | That is how top light works. For a flattering face switch to `side-light`, or move the source 30° forward |

## Examples

No cut in *The Letter Back* is top-lit. **That is worth stating plainly, because it very nearly is**: the only key indoors is the bare bulb over the round table, hanging directly above the two men. Every physical condition is in place.

The film declines to press it down. Every cut of the sitting sequence, R08 through R17, also carries cold fill from the blue glass window in the back wall, and the film's art baseline has exactly one hard rule about light: shadows always keep detail. With that fill present the sockets, the underside of the nose and the underside of the chin cannot sink, and not one identifying feature of top light survives. The closest cut is R18, after dark, with only the ring of light from the bulb left: shadow takes most of the frame, but that is low key rather than top light — the pool lands on the lit half of each face and on their hands on the wood, not pressing down from the crown.

To attach this card to this story, the place is R11, the over-the-shoulder where the father speaks: strike the blue-window fill from that prompt, write the light as coming from directly above with the eye sockets still readable, and give the camera its own clause at eye level. The father's sockets go dark, the son keeps his window fill, and **from then on the two are not in the same light** — who is pressing and who is retreating stops needing dialogue. The film does not do it, for exactly the opposite reason: it wants these two sitting in one light all afternoon.
