## What it is

The subject sits dead in the middle of the frame with equal weight either side. **The difference from symmetry is jurisdiction**: symmetry demands that the two halves of the whole image mirror each other, so the background is half the job; centred framing only cares where the subject sits and lets the background be a mess. **The difference from thirds is direction**: centred framing flattens direction out, leaving the audience one thing to do — look at the subject.

The one-line test: **this cut says "this one", use centre; it says "they are headed somewhere", use thirds.**

Translated into words a model obeys: never write `centered composition` — the model files it under vague style. Write "the figure's spine runs down the centre line of the frame, equal distance to both edges, and it does not drift".

## When to use it

- **The opening cut of a talking head.** Presenter square to the lens, planted in the middle, and the first second tells the viewer where to look. It is the default cut for the format and the worst place to get clever.
- **Authority and judgement.** Ring someone with figures and put the surrounded one dead centre — the straighter the framing, the less escape there is. Flip it and centre the interrogator, and the pressure radiates out of the middle of the frame.
- **Solitude in a big empty place.** A figure centred in a large location, facing camera or away from it. There is no direction anywhere, so the audience simply waits for them to move.
- **Faces at extreme close size.** When the face fills the frame, centred is the default, with the eyes a touch above the midline.
- **The default layout for vertical talking head and vertical product.** In 9:16 the frame is too narrow for anything else to hold horizontally; the knob that actually matters is vertical placement.

**When not to use it**:

- **Not when the subject is walking or looking at someone off-frame.** Centring erases the direction and the scene loses where it was going.
- **Not for the singles in a shot-reverse.** Centre both and the audience cannot read the left-right relationship — you have crossed the line on yourself.
- **Not when two figures are unequal.** Centring levels them, and the power difference has to be carried entirely by dialogue.
- **Not when the cut needs to lead somewhere.** Centred framing is a full stop, not a comma.

## How to prompt it

One cut is the norm. Centred product belongs to `product-orbit`, which carries its own required phrases — do not stack the two cards.

```
medium shot, subject dead centre in frame（the figure's spine on the centre line）,
centre stays fixed for the whole cut（no drift across the cut）,
what the subject is doing on the spot, what sits left and right（named item by
item, comparable density）, background and lighting state
```

- **Write placement, not vocabulary.** Swap `centered composition` for `the figure's spine runs down the centre line of the frame` and compliance goes up by an order of magnitude.
- **Name both sides.** Ask for centred without describing left and right and the model routinely piles one side high and leaves the other bare — the result does not read as centred, it reads as misaligned.
- **In 9:16 the knob that matters is vertical.** Horizontal centring happens by itself in a narrow frame; what is worth pinning is where the figure falls in the height. Default is eyes at 40% down the frame with the chest or waist line at the bottom edge. Do not let them land exactly halfway up or a slab of headroom opens and the figure reads short.
- **The centre is easiest to lose under a move.** `Push In` and `Zoom In` slowly slide the subject off the middle, which is exactly what `centre stays fixed for the whole cut` exists to stop.
- **The `[Shot k]` passage covers what the subject does on the spot.** The moment they travel sideways, centred framing dismantles itself — if they have to walk, switch to the thirds card.

## Parameters

| Knob | Default | Range | How it feels when you tune it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal offset | 0 | within 5% of frame width | Inside 5% still reads centred; past 10% it reads as misaligned, which looks far worse than a committed thirds offset |
| Vertical position (9:16) | eyes 40% down | 30%–50% | Higher tightens headroom and raises the pressure; at 50% a slab of headroom opens and the figure reads short and inert |
| Subject's share of frame height | one half | one third to three quarters | Small is ritual and solitude, large is pressure and confrontation; past three quarters the location disappears |
| Density difference between sides | roughly equal | equal to slightly uneven | Perfectly equal slides into symmetry; slightly uneven feels shot rather than rendered, and stops the model mirroring the background |
| Seconds per cut | 4s | 2–6s | Centred frames hold well and can run long; past 6s of stillness the audience starts to think the video has frozen |

## Known pitfalls

| Symptom | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow drift | The subject slides off centre within one cut, worst on push-ins | `centre stays fixed for the whole cut` is mandatory; also shorten the push |
| Lopsided | You asked for centred and got props stacked left, nothing right — it reads offset | Name both sides' contents and state that their density is comparable |
| Headroom slab | In 9:16 half the frame is above the head and the figure is jammed at the bottom | Pin the vertical: state that the eyes sit 40% down the frame |
| Accidental symmetry | You only wanted the subject centred and the model mirrored the whole background | State explicitly that `the background does not need to match on both sides` |
| Passport photos | Several centred cuts in a row and the sequence reads like an ID gallery | Use centring as an accent — one or two cuts per scene, the rest on thirds and foreground occlusion |

## Examples

*The Letter Back* R26: the red enamel flask pinned dead centre, its axis on the vertical midline, the centre fixed for the whole cut on a locked-off camera with no drift. Behind it the wainscot line crosses at the same height left and right, the room sunk dark, one bare bulb warm from above and a cold edge from the window. Four seconds, medium.

**This cut sits one step short of symmetry, and the boundary is exactly there**: it pins the subject in the middle and never asks the two halves to mirror each other — the wall happening to agree is a gift from the location, not a requirement of the card. Worth noting too that this is a product cut: centred plus locked-off is the cheapest combination in the library for consistency, with no travel, no turn, and nothing to let the subject wander off the midline mid-cut.
