## What it is

The camera position never changes; only the focal length does. Narrowing the field of view is `Zoom In`, widening it is `Zoom Out`.

**This and the dolly push (`push-in`) are the pair this vocabulary confuses most, and confusing them is expensive.** Travelling the body forward and lengthening the lens give completely different pictures:

- **Push** — the position changes, so perspective changes, the subject magnifies faster than the background, the gap between subject and background opens, and the audience feels *I walked over there*.
- **Zoom** — the position holds, perspective is frozen, everything in frame magnifies at the same rate, the background is cropped narrower and narrower until it looks pasted behind the subject, and the audience feels *now I can see it*.

The one-line test: **a push changes the relationship, a zoom only changes the framing.** For emotion, go to `push-in`. For information, stay here.

A zoom carries a second identity: **it is a move the audience can hear.** A slow push is invisible; a zoom of any speed announces that a lens is being operated, which lends it a documentary, surveillance, caught-on-camera flavour. That flavour is a resource, not a defect — but it will sit on top of the drama.

## When to use it

- **A snap zoom as punctuation.** The joke lands, half a second of hard tightening, and the comedy is there. This is the one thing a zoom does that a push cannot.
- **Documentary distance, the sense of being watched.** Observe two people talking from far off, then zoom once mid-conversation and the audience instantly registers that somebody is looking at them.
- **Pointing in a vlog.** You mention a thing, you zoom to the thing. No emotion required, just deixis.
- **When you want long-lens compression as the outcome.** Zooming in crops the background narrow so it crowds up behind the subject; a person in a crowd looks more hemmed in. Want that crush, zoom; want depth, do not.
- **A subject you cannot get near.** A boat out on the water, a figure on a roof, something dangerous. The camera cannot go there, the lens can.

**When not to use it**:

- **Not when you want the audience to feel something.** A zoom is a change of framing, not an approach. Give that cut a `Push In`.
- **Not in straight dramatic dialogue.** A zoom makes serious material read as light entertainment; what the audience notices is the filming, not the event.
- **Not when depth is the point.** Zooming out gives a flat space — the frame widens but nothing recedes.
- **Never stacked with a camera move in the same cut.** Zoom plus truck, zoom plus push, and the model mashes two logics into collapsed perspective. If you genuinely want that effect it is a dolly-zoom, which is not this card's business.
- **Not as a slow zoom past 3 seconds.** Slow and long gets you neither the push's embodiment nor the zoom's snap, and it still announces the lens.

## How to prompt it

One cut, short. **State that the camera does not move** — leave that out and the model hands you a push nine times out of ten.

```
wide shot at the start（where the subject sits in frame, what surrounds them）,
the lens tightens while the camera stays put（what size it ends on — e.g. ends framed at the head and shoulders）,
all elements scale together（subject and background magnify at the same rate）,
perspective stays unchanged（position held, spatial relationships frozen）, lighting state
```

For the widening direction, swap the second line for `the lens widens while the camera stays put` and leave the rest alone.

- **`perspective stays unchanged` is the load-bearing phrase.** Without it the model defaults to reading the cut as a push, and you get a counterfeit dolly.
- **`the camera stays put` needs to appear once in its own right.** The must-phrase states the result on screen, this states the cause; the model holds the idea better with both.
- **Start at least one size step wider than you end.** All of a zoom's readability comes from the amount; too little and the audience just thinks the picture twitched.
- **Say `fast` or `slow` — never leave it blank.** Blank gets you medium, and a medium-speed zoom is the one with no personality.
- **The `[Shot k]` passage says only that the focal length is changing**, and restates that the camera holds position. Give either `Zoom In` or `Zoom Out`, never both.

## Parameters

| Knob | Default | Range | How it feels when you tune it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seconds per cut | 2s | 1–3s | A zoom is punctuation, not a sentence. 1s is the snap zoom, for comedy and shocks; past 3s it just looks like the picture is slowly swelling and the beat is gone |
| Amount | one size step | one to two steps | Less than a step is invisible and reads as a twitch; two steps hits hardest and is likeliest to expose missing detail at the end |
| Speed | moderate | snap to slow | Snap brings the comic and documentary flavour; a slow zoom should either be very slow and uneasy, or not happen |
| Starting size | wide | extreme wide to medium | The wider the start, the more legible the same amount; if you are already close, do not zoom |
| Zooms per scene | 1 | 1–2 | Past two the audience starts counting camera moves and the drama becomes a recording |

## Known pitfalls

| Symptom | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turned into a push | You asked for a zoom and the background falls away with real recession — the camera clearly moved | `perspective stays unchanged` and `all elements scale together`, both, plus `the camera stays put` |
| Too little travel | The picture appears to swell slightly and the audience reads it as camera shake | Start one step wider, or end one step tighter, so the move spans a full size step |
| Mush at the end | The features, lettering or texture at the end of the zoom are a smear | A zoom is a crop, so the end detail has to come from reference: hang the subject's sheet and name the two or three details visible at the end |
| Edge stretching | Snap zooms produce rubber-band smearing and ghosting in the corners | Drop the speed a notch, or replace the cut with two hard-cut frames (start size, end size) — a hard cut looks better than a broken snap zoom |
| Double motion | Zoom and a lateral move in one cut, and the perspective collapses | One motion per cut. If you want both, that is dolly-zoom territory — do not improvise it here |

## Examples

A general-purpose card; *The Letter Back* never uses one. Its thirty-six cuts draw on nine camera words — `Static Shot`, `Tracking Shot`, `Push In`, `Pull Out`, `Truck Right`, `Pan Right`, `Tilt Up`, `Arc Shot`, `POV` — and not one of them is a zoom. That is not an oversight. The film is two men at a table, its emotion is staked on the single push at R14, and **a push changes the relationship while a zoom only changes the framing**. This kind of scene wants the former.

The nearest reference point is R29: a long lens from across the room flattening the tabletop, the flask, the wall calendar and the blue window into one plane, with the depth of the room gone. That cut is a static long lens, not a zoom — but it shows precisely what the picture looks like at the end of a `Zoom In`. **The zoom is the journey; R29 is where it lands.**

The card's signature cut looks like this: camera bolted in place, focal length narrowing, everything in frame enlarging together, perspective untouched, the background cropped tighter and tighter until it seems pasted onto the subject's back — and the audience knowing, unmistakably, that the lens is doing something. Its home is the punchline in short-form video, the pointing gesture in a vlog, the observed-from-somewhere feel of documentary and surveillance footage. Not straight dramatic dialogue.
